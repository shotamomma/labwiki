## Cognitive psychologists

### Priming the structure of sentences
[[Priming]] is a useful tool for studying our cognition. In the domain of psycholinguistics, there is a sub-species of priming effects known as [[syntactic priming]] or [[structural priming]]. This paper uses this effect to probe how (English) speakers represent sentence structures in the mind.

What is syntactic priming? When you want to convey some meaning by speaking a sentence, often there are more than one way to do so. For example:

1. The girl gave the boy the book.
2. The girl gave the book to the boy.

These sentences are nearly synonymous. (1) is called [[double object dative]] and the other is called [[prepositional object dative]], and this alternation pattern is known as [[dative alternation]] in linguistics. If either version works for expressing what you mean, you have to make a choice as a speaker, and this choice is influenced by sentences you encountered prior to speaking; if you've recently encountered the (unrelated) double object dative sentence or a prepositional object dative sentences, you are more likely to use, for your own sentence, the version you encountered. This is [[syntactic priming]].

Syntactic priming can be observed with varieties of meaning-preserving syntactic alternations. For the current purposes, the most relevant alternation is English [[complementizer]] alternation.

>[!summary] **Structural priming**
> Speakers/signers can be primed to use either structure of a (roughly) meaning-equivalent structural altenration by exposing them to a (potentially completely unrelated) sentence with the same structure.

### Priming complementizer alternation

In linguistics, [[complementizer]] is a small function word that introduces a clause. In English, the most typical complementizer is *that*, as in the following sentence:

- *The girl thinks **that** the boy likes the dog.*

Notice that this sentence can be said without 'that' as well without changing (much) the meaning.

- *The girl thinks the boy likes the dog.*

So as a speaker you have a choice to make: you can use *that* or not in your sentence. This choice can be structurally primed. I call this the complementizer priming effect.

>[!summary] **Complementizer priming**
> Speakers can be primed to use 'that' or the null complementizer. This **complementizer priming** effect occurs:
> 1. Across seemingly disparate constructions (e.g., relative clauses to complement clauses)
> 2. In both directions - both *that* and the null complementizer can be primed, compared to the neutral baseline.
